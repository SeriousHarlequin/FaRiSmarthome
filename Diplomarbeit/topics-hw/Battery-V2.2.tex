\subsection{Hardware - V2.2(Final)}

    This section describes the hardware of the final version of the battery module.

    \subsubsection{Module-Connector}
        The basic module is plugged into the battery module with the pins 1-5. 
        The battery module is connected to the following voltages:

        \begin{itemize}
            \item 1. VBAT - used to detect battery and monitor the battery voltage
            \item 2. VBUS - used to charge and disable the battery module when the USB-C 
            is connected
            \item 3. 3V3 - not used, but may be relevant for future modules/revisions
            \item 4. GND
            \item 5. 5V - The battery module powers the basic node over this pin
        \end{itemize}
        
        With the voltages the basic node is powered, and the battery is charged.

    \subsubsection{Battery}

        The battery module uses a 3.7V Li-Ion battery to power the basic node. The battery can 
        be any Li-Ion battery. The battery is secured with a 1A fuse.
 
        \begin{figure}[H]
            \centering

            \caption{Battery implemented in the schematic.}
        \end{figure}

    \subsubsection{Battery Management System}

        The BMS handles the charging of the battery and assures that the battery is not
        being overcharged. The used IC is the MCP73811\cite{noauthor_mcp73811_2007} from Microchip, it is a linear 
        charge management controller for both Li-Ion and Li-Polymer batteries. 
        To prevent over-discharging, the microcontroller measures the battery voltage
        and can goes into deep sleep mode when the battery voltage is too low.

    \subsubsection{Boost Converter}

        The boost converter is used to convert the 3.7V from the battery to 5V to power the 
        basic node. The used IC is the TLV61070 from Texas Instruments, it is a high efficiency
        boost converter with a 95\% efficiency.

        The inductor and the output voltage were calculated like stated in the
        datasheet\cite{noauthor_tlv61070apdf_2022}. The inductor was calculated with the formula:
        
        \begin{equation}
            I{L(DC)} = \frac{V_{OUT} \cdot I_{OUT}}{V_{IN} \cdot \eta}
        \end{equation}   

            
        Where $I_{OUT}$ is the output current, $V_{IN}$ is the input voltage (worst case) and $\eta$ is the
        efficiency(typical 90\%) of the boost converter. The output current was set to 1A, the input voltage 
        to 3V and the output voltage to 5V. 

        \begin{equation}
            I_{L(DC)} = \frac{5V \cdot 1A}{3V \cdot 0.9} = \textbf{1.85A}
        \end{equation}
        \newline

        The output voltage was set with a resistor divider to 5V:

        \begin{equation}
            R1 = ( \frac{V_{OUT}}{V_{REF}} - 1) \cdot R2
        \end{equation}

        Where $V_{REF}$ \cite{noauthor_tlv61070apdf_2022} is 500mV and $V_{OUT}$ is 5V. R2 was set to 100kOhm.

        \begin{equation}
            R1 = ( \frac{5V}{500mV} - 1) \cdot 100k\Omega = 900k\Omega \approx \mathbf{910M\Omega(E24)}
        \end{equation}

        The closest value to \(900k\Omega\) was \(910k\Omega\) in the E24 series.
        
        To prevent the boost converter from operating when the USB-C port is connected, the
        VBUS voltage is used to disable the boost converter. When a charger is connected
        the VBUS signal(5V) it switches the transistor Q1 and pulls the enable pin to ground 
        and disabling it.

    \subsubsection{ON/OFF Switch}
        The switch disconnects the battery from the boost converter. Note that the battery 
        charging circuit is not influenced since VBUS is directly connected to the BMS as seen
        in figure \ref{fig:battery-chargerIC}.

    \subsubsection{PCB - V2.2}

        The PCB has a size of 30x41.5mm, it fits directly under the basic module. 
        The components used were mainly SMD components, in order to keep the PCB small. 
        It is a 2-layer PCB.
